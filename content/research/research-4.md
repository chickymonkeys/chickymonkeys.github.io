Among the causes of the French Revolution, a "folk hypothesis" holds that the 1783 eruption of Laki, in Iceland, contributed to unrest in the revolutionary years. We present the first empirical evidence on this hypothesis and on how the eruption influenced the nature of the Revolution. The sinking air of an anticyclone brought the haze of the eruption down to the ground unevenly in France. Comparing the places more and less exposed, before and after 1783, we show that the more exposed places faced a larger rise in the price of grain, following the harsh winter after the eruption. Dear grain turned into protests. The same places had more subsistence riots after the eruption and again in the early years of the Revolution, above all in the towns, where people bought their grain. As grain riots spread, the Third Estate wrote its grievances in the _cahiers de doléances_ and elected its deputies to the _Estates-General_. The more exposed places moved away from its liberal programme, putting food before representation. Fewer of their deputies were men of the law, who led the Third Estate. Their parishes asked less often for the vote by head, leaning toward the regulation of the trade in grain. However, the general _cahiers_ of their districts, drawn up by local notables, were less likely to defend the _Ancien Régime_. As the eruption changed what the Revolution was about in the places it hit hardest, our results broadly suggest that natural disasters carry political consequences as well as economic ones, with implications for societal stability as exteme weather events become more frequent.
