This paper shows that credit shocks are key drivers of populist sentiment. Exploiting spatial variation in exposure to an exogenous lending cut by a large German bank in 2007-08, we find that exposure to the shock causes a persistent increase in populist political preferences. Using machine learning applied to German parliamentary speeches, we show that the shift favours parties combining populist rhetoric with attention to banking issues. County-level evidence suggests that the mechanism operates through voters' perceptions of local economic decline. A decomposition of individual causal effects highlights labour market history as the main factor shaping voters' populist reaction to the credit shock.
